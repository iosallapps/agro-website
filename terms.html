2. What Agrondo does

Agrondo runs on iOS, in 16 languages. This is all of it:

  • Draw field and parcel boundaries on a satellite map and read the area back in hectares or acres.
  • Keep a farm journal: crops sown, treatments with the dose and the date, field operations, harvests and photos.
  • Record cost and revenue per parcel and per field, and see the profit per hectare.
  • Get a warning when the same crop comes back to a parcel in consecutive years.
  • See a weather forecast for the location you mapped.
  • Identify a plant from a photo, backed by PlantNet, and read a care library covering 61 species.
  • Set reminders that arrive as notifications.
  • Export reports as PDF and CSV.

Your records are kept on the device rather than on a server, which is why the app works in a field with no signal. If something is not in that list, the app does not do it, and nothing on this site or in the App Store listing should be read as a promise that it will. 7. Plant identification can be wrong

Photograph a plant and Agrondo offers a name. That answer comes from PlantNet, run by a French research consortium, and it is a best guess made from one image. A poor angle, flat light, a young plant or a leaf covered in dust are all enough to make it wrong.

The care library covering 61 species is general reading. It is written for the species, not for your soil, your season or your parcel.

Do not spray, pull, drill, plant or feed anything on the strength of an identification alone. Confirm it with an agronomist or your own eye first. Never use the app to decide whether a plant is safe to eat or safe to feed to animals. Agrondo gives you information. It does not give agronomic advice, and it is not a recommendation for any plant protection product.

8. The weather forecast

The forecast comes from Open-Meteo, a third party service, for the coordinates of the map you are looking at. A forecast is a probability. It can be wrong about rain, wind and frost, and it cannot tell you what is happening in one particular field at one particular hour.

Judge the spray window, the cut and the drilling date on the ground. Look at the sky, and check whatever the rules where you farm require you to check. If the service is down or the numbers are stale, that is the forecast provider, and we carry no liability for what the weather then did.

9. Your records are yours to keep

Your fields, journal entries, photos and figures stay on the phone. We hold no copy of them, so there is nothing we can restore for you. Four things follow from that, and they are your responsibility:

  • Back the phone up. The app does not sync to iCloud at present, so your records live on the one device that made them. Use your normal iPhone backup. A lost phone with no backup is lost records.
  • Check what you type. The app adds up the figures you give it. A dose or a price entered wrong comes out the far end as a wrong report.
  • Area comes from the boundary you trace. It is a measurement off a map, not a cadastral survey. It does not replace the land registry figure or a licensed surveyor, and it should not be used to settle a boundary with a neighbour.
  • Exports leave your hands. Once you send a PDF or a CSV to an accountant, an agronomist or a buyer, where it goes next is between you and them.

Two things do leave the phone, and only when you ask for them: the single photo you send for an identification, and the coordinates the forecast needs. The privacy policy covers both, line by line.

It is not a compliance system, it is not certified to any scheme, and it makes no declaration to any authority on your behalf.

Before you rely on the app at an inspection or in a subsidy claim, check that what it stores and what it exports satisfies your own authority. Where the app and the law disagree, the law wins. Rotation warnings read your own history back to you. They are not a check against a greening rule or a scheme condition.
